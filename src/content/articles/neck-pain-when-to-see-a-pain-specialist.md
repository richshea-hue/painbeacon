Almost everyone gets neck pain at some point. You wake up and cannot turn your
head, or a dull ache builds through a week at a desk. Most of the time it fades
on its own, which is why "give it a couple of weeks" is honest advice and not a
brush-off.

The harder questions come later. How long is too long? What does it mean when
the pain runs into an arm? And when is it time to see a pain specialist?

## Most neck pain gets better without a specialist

A new episode of neck pain with no injury behind it usually comes from the
muscles, ligaments and small joints of the neck. It tends to improve over a few
days to a few weeks. What helps in that window is unglamorous:

- **Keep moving.** Gentle, normal movement does better than holding the neck
  still. Soft collars are not recommended for ordinary neck pain.
- **Heat or ice**, whichever feels better.
- **Over-the-counter pain relievers**, if they are safe for you. Ask a
  pharmacist or your doctor if you take other medicines or have kidney, stomach
  or heart conditions.

If the pain is clearly easing week by week, you are on the usual course.

## Go now, not next week

A few situations should not wait for an appointment. Get emergency or same-day
care for neck pain that comes with:

- **A serious injury.** A car crash, a fall or a blow to the head or neck.
- **Fever, a severe headache and a stiff neck together**, especially with
  sensitivity to light or confusion.
- **New weakness, numbness or clumsiness in the arms or hands, trouble
  walking, or new loss of bladder or bowel control.** These can mean pressure
  on the spinal cord.
- **Sudden, severe neck pain with stroke signs**: a drooping face, slurred
  speech, vision changes, or sudden dizziness and loss of balance.
- **Chest pain or pressure, shortness of breath or sweating.** Pain from the
  heart can be felt in the neck, jaw or arm.

Call your doctor promptly, though not necessarily the same day, if neck pain
comes with unexplained weight loss, a history of cancer, or pain that is
steadily worse at night and at rest.

## When the pain runs down an arm

Pain that shoots from the neck into the shoulder, arm or hand, often with
tingling or numbness in particular fingers, usually means a nerve root is
irritated where it leaves the spine. Doctors call this cervical radiculopathy.
Most people know it as a pinched nerve. A bulging disc or age-related narrowing
around the nerve is the usual cause.

It is the neck's version of
[sciatica](/news/sciatica-when-to-see-a-specialist/), and it follows a similar
course: it can be very painful, and most cases improve over weeks to a few
months without surgery. See a clinician to confirm what it is. The thing to
watch for is weakness. An arm or hand that is getting weaker, not just sore,
needs to be seen quickly.

## When a pain specialist makes sense

Your primary care clinician is the right first stop for neck pain that has
lasted more than a couple of weeks without improving. Physical therapy is
usually the next step, and for most lasting neck pain it has better evidence
behind it than any injection.

A pain clinic becomes worth considering when:

- **The pain has lasted around three months or more** despite physical therapy
  and medication.
- **Arm pain from a pinched nerve is not settling** after several weeks, or is
  too severe to let you sleep, work or do the therapy.
- **Neck pain has lingered after a whiplash injury** long past the point where
  the strain should have healed.
- **Headaches start at the base of the skull** and seem tied to the neck.
- **You have been told surgery is not needed or not advisable**, or you still
  have pain after neck surgery.

## What a pain clinic can do for a neck

A first visit is mostly questions and a hands-on exam; our guide to
[your first pain clinic appointment](/news/first-pain-clinic-appointment/)
covers what to bring. The options then depend on where the pain comes from.

**Facet joint pain.** The small joints at the back of the neck are a common
source of long-running neck pain and of some neck-related headaches. Clinics
test for this by numbing the tiny nerves that serve the joint, a diagnostic
step explained in our guide to
[nerve blocks](/news/nerve-blocks-diagnostic-vs-therapeutic/). If the test
clearly helps, [radiofrequency ablation](/news/radiofrequency-ablation-back-neck-pain/)
can quiet those nerves for longer.

**Arm pain from a pinched nerve.** A cervical epidural steroid injection places
anti-inflammatory medicine near the irritated nerve. The evidence supports
short-term relief of arm pain for some people. It is weaker for pain felt only
in the neck. Serious complications are rare, and they are real: in 2014 the
FDA required a warning about rare but serious neurologic events with epidural
steroid injections. Ask who will do the injection and what imaging guides the
needle. Our guide to
[epidural steroid injections](/news/epidural-steroid-injections-evidence/)
covers the research.

A clinic should also review your medications, your sleep and your physical
therapy plan. A place that offers only procedures is offering half of pain
care.

## What a scan can and cannot tell you

Without red flags, neck pain in its first several weeks usually does not need
an MRI. Disc bulges and
age-related changes show up on the scans of a large share of adults who feel
fine, and they become more common with every decade. A finding on a report is
not automatically the cause of your pain.

Imaging earns its place when symptoms persist, when there is weakness or
another warning sign, or when a procedure or surgery is being planned.

## When a surgeon is the right call

Most neck pain is never a surgical problem. A referral to a spine surgeon is
appropriate for signs of spinal cord pressure, for weakness that is getting
worse, and for arm pain that has not improved after a fair trial of other
treatment and matches a clear finding on a scan. Seeing a surgeon is a
consultation, not a commitment.

## Finding a pain clinic for neck pain

When you [search for pain clinics near you](/pain-clinics/), one signal in
[how we rank](/how-we-rank/) is whether a practice has registered under
interventional pain medicine, which points to a focus on image-guided
procedures. That registration is declared by the practice itself. It is not a
certification, and our rankings do not yet track which procedures each practice
performs. So call two or three clinics and ask: Do you treat neck pain? Do you
perform cervical procedures regularly? What do you try before an injection?

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Neck pain has many
causes, some of them urgent, and only a licensed clinician who has examined you
can tell you which one you have and what treatment is appropriate.*
