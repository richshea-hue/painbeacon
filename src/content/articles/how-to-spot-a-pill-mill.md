"Pill mill" is a legal and journalistic term, not a medical one. It describes a
practice whose actual business is writing prescriptions in volume — where the
appointment exists to justify the script rather than to diagnose anything. These
practices are rare, and they have gotten rarer as prescribing oversight has
tightened. But they still open, and they market themselves in exactly the same
words a legitimate pain clinic uses.

The cost of walking into one isn't only legal. You lose months to a clinic that
was never going to find out what is causing your pain, you may end up on
medication that doesn't fit your condition, and if the practice is later shut
down, you can be left without records, without a prescriber, and with a chart
that makes the next physician cautious about taking you on. It is worth twenty
minutes of checking before you book.

## What you can check from home

Most of the warning signs are visible before you ever call.

**No named physicians.** A legitimate practice publishes who works there. If the
website shows stock photography, a phone number, and no clinician names you can
look up, that is the single strongest signal on this list.

**Advertising that leads with medication.** Real pain clinics advertise
conditions and capabilities — sciatica, back pain, image-guided procedures.
Language centered on prescriptions, specific drug names, "medication refills," or
promises like "no records needed" and "same-day scripts" is describing a product,
not a practice.

**Cash only, no insurance billed.** Legitimate clinics bill insurance because
insurance pays them. A pain practice that takes no insurance at all, quotes a
flat per-visit fee regardless of what happens in the visit, and prefers cash is
avoiding the documentation that billing requires. (Cash-pay concierge medicine
does exist — but it is unusual in pain management, and worth a direct question.)

**No interest in your records.** A clinic that will book you without a referral,
prior imaging, or a records release is skipping the part where a diagnosis comes
from. Compare that with a [thorough first
appointment](/news/first-pain-clinic-appointment/), which is mostly history and
examination.

**Volume signals.** Appointment slots always available today, at any hour, at a
practice with a single physician. Patients traveling long distances or from
across state lines. A waiting room that runs like a queue.

**A quick public-records pass.** Three lookups cover most of it: your state
medical board's license search, which shows license status and any disciplinary
action; the federal NPI registry, which shows how the practice is actually
registered; and a plain search of the clinic and physician's name alongside words
like "indicted," "settlement," or "board action." Federal and state enforcement
actions are public and easy to find.

## Red flags inside the visit

Some signals only appear once you're in the room. Any one of these is worth
asking about; several together is worth leaving over.

- **No physical examination.** A pain evaluation that never involves examining
  you is not an evaluation.
- **The prescription was decided before the exam.** If the medication comes up
  first and the diagnosis second, the order is backwards.
- **No discussion of anything but medication.** A practice that never mentions
  physical therapy, injections, or any [non-drug
  option](/news/what-interventional-pain-clinics-do/) has one tool.
- **A five-minute visit.** Volume is the business model of a pill mill, and it
  shows up in the clock.
- **Cash pressure for a procedure**, or an upsell to a procedure at the first
  visit with no diagnostic reasoning behind it.

## The signs that look strict but are actually good

This is where patients most often misread a clinic. Legitimate pain practices —
especially those managing controlled substances — will typically ask you to sign
a treatment agreement, submit to periodic urine drug screening, use a single
pharmacy, agree to pill counts, and accept that the physician checks your state's
prescription drug monitoring program before writing anything. Many will also
require in-person visits for refills.

None of that is a red flag. It is the opposite: it is what careful prescribing
looks like, and it is a reason to trust the practice more, not less. A clinic
that skips all of it is not being generous with you.

Similarly, being prescribed opioids at all is not a warning sign. Opioids remain
an appropriate part of care for some patients and some conditions. The question
is never whether medication is on the table — it is whether a diagnosis came
first.

## If you are already a patient somewhere that fits this profile

Do not stop taking prescribed medication abruptly on your own — abrupt
discontinuation of opioids or other controlled medications carries real risk.
Instead, ask for a complete copy of your records now while the practice is
operating, tell your primary care physician what you are taking and why, and ask
for a referral to a board-certified pain physician who can take over the
prescribing safely. Transitions like this are routine; the receiving clinic will
have handled them before.

## The short version

One unusual thing is rarely the answer. A cluster is. A practice with no named
clinicians, no insurance billing, no interest in your records, and no plan beyond
a prescription is telling you what it is, in four separate ways, before you ever
walk in.
