At some point in a long pain journey, someone in a white coat proposes doing
something to you rather than just prescribing something for you: an
injection, a nerve ablation, an implanted device. The conversation often
happens fast, near the end of a visit, with a consent form already printed.
That pace is the problem. A procedure is a bigger decision than a
prescription, and it deserves a slower conversation before you agree to it.

None of the questions below are about doubting your clinician. They are
about making sure the two of you are actually discussing the same thing —
your specific pain, your specific anatomy, your specific goals — rather than
a generic version of the procedure that happens to be on the schedule that
week.

## Start with the diagnosis, not the procedure

The single most useful question is also the most basic one: **what exactly
is this treating, and how do you know?** A good answer connects your
symptoms, your exam, and your imaging into one story. A weak answer jumps
straight from "your MRI shows X" to "so we'll do Y," without explaining why
X is actually causing what you feel.

Imaging finds things in a lot of people who have no pain at all, so a
finding on a scan is not automatically the source of your symptoms. Ask
what in your exam supports that this specific finding is the cause, and
what else was considered and ruled out. Our look at [what the evidence
actually says about epidural steroid injections](/news/epidural-steroid-injections-evidence/)
is a good example of how much the answer depends on your exact pattern:
the same procedure that reliably helps leg pain from a pinched nerve does
much less for pain that stays in the back.

Two follow-up questions belong in the same breath: **what happens if I do
nothing, and what have we already tried that didn't work?** A procedure
proposed before more conservative options have had a real trial is worth a
second opinion.

## Ask what the evidence says about someone like you

"It works" is not a complete answer, because most pain procedures work
well for some patterns and poorly for others. Ask what the evidence shows
specifically for your diagnosis, not the procedure in general, and ask how
your clinician defines success — a specific percentage of pain relief, a
return to a specific activity, less reliance on medication. Vague success
criteria make it hard to know afterward whether the procedure actually
worked.

It is also fair to ask how long relief typically lasts, and whether the
procedure is a one-time fix, something usually repeated, or a step toward a
bigger one. Some procedures, like a spinal cord stimulator, are designed to
be tested before you commit to anything permanent — our guide to [how a
stimulator trial works](/news/spinal-cord-stimulator-trial/) is a useful
model for the kind of "try it and see" question worth asking about other
devices and injections, too: is there a smaller, reversible version of this
I can attempt first?

## Ask about risk in plain numbers

Every procedure carries some chance of things going wrong, and a clinician
who can discuss that plainly, without minimizing or over-selling, is
generally more trustworthy than one who calls it "basically no risk."
Reasonable questions:

- **What are the common side effects, and how long do they usually last?**
- **What are the rare but serious risks, and how often do they actually
  happen at this practice?**
- **What does recovery look like — time off work, activity restrictions,
  driving?**
- **If something goes wrong, who handles it, and how quickly can I reach
  someone?**

That last one matters more than it sounds. A practice that can answer
clearly, by name or by role, is telling you something about how it is run.
A practice that shrugs is telling you something too.

## Ask who is actually doing it, and how often

Experience is one of the more honestly answerable questions in medicine,
and clinicians who do a lot of a given procedure tend to have fewer
complications and better outcomes than those who do it occasionally. It is
reasonable to ask **how many of these you perform, and how often do you
need to revise, repeat, or remove them?** — the same question we suggested
for stimulator trials applies just as well to injections, ablations, and
implants generally.

It is also worth asking whether the person performing the procedure is the
one you have been talking to, or someone else in the practice, and what
their training and credentials are for this specific procedure. Our guide
to [choosing a pain clinic](/news/how-to-choose-a-pain-clinic/) covers the
credentials worth checking before you ever book a consultation, and the
same questions apply again here, one level more specifically, before you
consent to something happening to your body.

## Ask what it costs, and who decided that

Cost questions get skipped more than any other category, usually because
they feel awkward to raise in a clinical conversation. Ask anyway, before
the day of the procedure rather than after: **is this covered by my
insurance, does it require prior authorization, and what will I owe if it
is only partially covered?** Our guide to [how insurance approvals and
coverage actually work](/news/does-insurance-cover-pain-management/) walks
through the referral and prior-authorization process most plans require,
and why the answer is rarely a flat yes or no.

A clinic that cannot or will not give you a straight answer about cost
before scheduling a procedure is not necessarily acting in bad faith —
billing is genuinely complicated — but you are entitled to push until you
get a real number, not a shrug toward the billing department.

## Ask what happens if it doesn't work

Not every procedure succeeds, and a good plan accounts for that in advance
rather than treating it as a surprise. Ask **what the next step is if this
doesn't help**, whether that means a different procedure, a referral
elsewhere, or accepting that this was the wrong tool for your pain. A
clinician who has a clear answer has actually thought past the procedure
itself. One who cannot answer at all may not have a plan beyond it.

## Putting it together

None of these questions require confrontation. Most can be asked plainly,
in the same visit where the procedure is proposed, and a clinician who
takes pain seriously should welcome them rather than rush past them. Bring
a written list if that helps you remember them under pressure — clinic
visits move fast, and the questions that matter most are easy to forget
once you are on the table.
