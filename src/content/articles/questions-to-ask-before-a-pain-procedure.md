Most people are not prepared for the moment a specialist says, "I'd like to
schedule an injection." The recommendation often arrives at the end of a short
visit, along with a consent form and a date. It can feel like the only choices
are yes and no.

There is a third choice: ask questions first. A good clinic expects them. The
eight below work for nearly any procedure, from an epidural steroid injection
to a radiofrequency ablation to a spinal cord stimulator trial. Bring them
written down, since it is easy to forget half of them when someone in a white
coat is waiting for your answer.

## 1. What exactly are you treating, and how do you know that is the source?

This is the most important question, and the one people skip. Procedures work
best when they are aimed at a specific, identified pain generator: an inflamed
nerve root, a particular joint, a defined nerve. Ask what finding led to this
recommendation. Was it your exam, your imaging, a diagnostic block, or a mix?

Imaging alone can mislead. Many people without any pain have disc bulges and
worn joints on a scan, so a scan is a clue, not a diagnosis. If the answer is
vague, you are entitled to ask for a clearer one before going ahead. Our look at
[how sciatica is evaluated](/news/sciatica-when-to-see-a-specialist/) shows why
the match between symptoms and findings matters.

## 2. What is the goal, and how will we know it worked?

"Help with the pain" is not a plan. Ask what a good result looks like for you:
less pain, yes, but also walking further, sleeping through the night, or
starting physical therapy you could not tolerate before. Ask how long relief is
typically expected to last, and what you should track afterward.

Many pain procedures are meant to create a window rather than a cure. Our
summary of [the evidence on epidural steroid injections](/news/epidural-steroid-injections-evidence/)
is a good example: the benefit for many people is real but temporary, which is
fine if everyone agrees that is the goal, and disappointing if nobody said so.

## 3. What are the alternatives, including doing nothing for now?

Ask what else could be tried first, and what happens if you wait. For some
problems, waiting and continuing conservative care is a sound choice. For others,
delay has a cost. Either way, you should hear both sides. This is also the moment
to ask how the procedure fits with the rest of your care, which is covered in our
guide to [pain management without opioids](/news/pain-management-without-opioids/).

## 4. What are the risks, and what are the ones that matter for me?

Every procedure has risks, and a good consent conversation names them in plain
words: what is common and minor, what is uncommon and serious, and what would
happen if something went wrong. Then make it personal. Tell the clinician about
any blood thinners, diabetes, allergies, prior reactions to contrast dye or
anesthesia, and pregnancy or the chance of it. Ask whether any of your
medications need to be adjusted beforehand. Do not stop a medication on your own.

## 5. Who will perform it, and how often do they do it?

Ask who is doing the procedure. Is it the physician you are meeting, or someone
else in the practice? Ask about their training and how often they perform this
particular procedure. You are not being rude. Experience matters more for some
procedures than others, and a confident clinician will answer without fuss.

## 6. What will the day look like?

Ask practical questions so nothing surprises you:

- Will I be awake, lightly sedated, or under anesthesia?
- Will imaging be used to guide the needle?
- Do I need a driver, and can I eat or drink beforehand?
- How long will I be there, and what are the limits on activity afterward?
- Which symptoms mean I should call, and who answers at night or on a weekend?

That last one is a good test of a practice. A clear after-hours plan is a sign of
a well-run office.

## 7. What will it cost, and will my insurance approve it?

Do not leave this until the bill arrives. Ask whether the procedure needs prior
authorization, whether the facility and anesthesia are billed separately, and what
your share is likely to be. The billing is often split among several parties.
Our guide to [whether insurance covers pain management](/news/does-insurance-cover-pain-management/)
walks through referrals, prior authorization and typical cost sharing. If the
office cannot give you even a rough estimate, ask who can.

## 8. What happens if it does not help?

Every procedure has a possible outcome where the pain does not change. Ask about
the next step now, while the plan is calm. Is there a repeat option, and how many
are reasonable? Is there a different procedure, or a return to other approaches?
Be wary of any plan that only ever repeats the same thing without a way to
judge whether it works.

## Signs to slow down

Take more time, or get a second opinion, if you notice any of the following:

- You are pressured to decide the same day, or told a slot will disappear.
- The same procedure is recommended to everyone, whatever their diagnosis.
- Questions are brushed aside, or risks are described as "nothing to worry about."
- You are asked to sign consent before anyone has explained the procedure.
- Nobody can say what the goal is or how success will be measured.

Our list of [red flags that suggest a pill mill](/news/spot-a-pill-mill-red-flags/)
covers a related set of warning signs at the practice level. A second opinion is
ordinary and reasonable, and clinics that are confident in their recommendation
do not resent it.

## Finding a clinic that welcomes questions

The directory can help you start. Our [directory of pain clinics](/pain-clinics/)
ranks practices on published, checkable signals, and [our methodology](/how-we-rank/)
lists exactly what those are: the interventional pain medicine credential, patient
ratings and review volume, years in practice, and whether a clinic publishes a
working website and phone number. It does not measure how a clinic handles
consent, and it does not track which procedures a practice offers or how often it
performs them. So a ranking is a place to begin, not a substitute for the
conversation above. Call, ask your questions, and notice how they are received.
