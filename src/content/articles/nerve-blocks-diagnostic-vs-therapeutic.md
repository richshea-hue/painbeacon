"Nerve block" is one of the most common phrases you will hear at a pain clinic,
and one of the least specific. It can mean a five-minute test that is supposed
to wear off by dinner, or a treatment meant to give you weeks of relief. The
needle and the numbing medicine can look identical. What differs is the
question the block is meant to answer, and that changes how you should judge
the result.

## What a nerve block is

A nerve block is an injection of local anesthetic, the same family of numbing
medicine a dentist uses, placed next to a specific nerve or cluster of nerves.
Sometimes a steroid is added to calm inflammation. Most blocks at a pain clinic
are guided by ultrasound or live X-ray so the medicine lands where it is meant
to, and most take a few minutes in an outpatient room.

Anesthesiologists also give nerve blocks before surgery. This article is about
blocks used for ongoing pain. For where they sit among other procedures, see
our overview of
[what an interventional pain clinic does](/news/what-interventional-pain-clinics-do/).

## Diagnostic blocks: a test, not a treatment

A diagnostic block answers one question: is this nerve carrying your pain?

Scans often cannot tell. Worn joints and bulging discs show up on the MRIs of
many people who have no pain at all. So the clinician numbs the suspected nerve
and watches what happens over the next few hours. If your usual pain fades
while the nerve is numb and returns when the medicine wears off, that nerve is
a likely source. If nothing changes, it probably is not, and that is useful to
know too.

Common diagnostic blocks include:

- **Medial branch blocks** for pain from the small facet joints of the spine.
- **Genicular nerve blocks** for knee pain.
- **Lateral branch blocks** for the sacroiliac joint, where the spine meets the
  pelvis.
- **Selective nerve root blocks**, sometimes used to work out which spinal
  level is causing arm or leg pain.

A diagnostic block is supposed to wear off. People often think it failed
because the pain was back the next morning. If the pain dropped sharply for a
few hours first, the test did exactly what it was for.

A positive result usually leads to a longer-lasting treatment aimed at the same
nerve, most often
[radiofrequency ablation](/news/radiofrequency-ablation-back-neck-pain/).

## How to make a diagnostic block count

The result depends on what you report, so your part matters.

- **Keep a pain log.** Rate your pain before the block and every hour or so
  afterward for the rest of the day.
- **Do the things that normally hurt.** If standing at the sink sets off your
  back, stand at the sink.
- **Ask about sedation and extra pain medication.** Either can blur the answer.
- **Be honest about a partial result.** Clinics and insurers usually look for a
  large drop in pain, commonly at least half and often more, not a vague
  "maybe a bit better."

A single block can mislead, because some people feel better after any
injection. That is why many insurers, including Medicare for spinal facet
joints, generally want two separate positive blocks before they cover an
ablation.

## Therapeutic blocks: the block is the treatment

A therapeutic block is given in the hope that the relief itself will last. The
anesthetic wears off in hours, but for some conditions the pain stays lower for
days, weeks or occasionally longer. How well this works depends heavily on the
condition, and the evidence is uneven.

Examples you may be offered:

- **Occipital nerve blocks** at the back of the head, for occipital neuralgia
  and some headache disorders.
- **Sympathetic blocks**, such as a stellate ganglion block in the neck or a
  lumbar sympathetic block in the low back, most often for complex regional
  pain syndrome. Research on how much they help is limited.
- **Intercostal blocks** between the ribs, for pain after rib injuries, chest
  surgery or shingles.
- **Celiac plexus blocks** for pain from pancreatic and other upper abdominal
  cancers, one of the better-studied uses.

Therapeutic blocks are often given as a short series. That is reasonable when
each one is checked against a goal, and less so when blocks continue every few
weeks with no clear benefit and no plan for what comes next.

## Why the difference matters

**It changes what success means.** For a diagnostic block, success is a clear
answer, even if the answer is "not this nerve." For a therapeutic block,
success is lasting relief and being able to do more.

**It changes what happens next.** A positive diagnostic block points to a
follow-up procedure. A therapeutic block that works may simply be repeated when
it wears off.

**It changes coverage.** Insurers often cap how many blocks they pay for in a
year and may need prior authorization. Our guide to
[insurance and pain management](/news/does-insurance-cover-pain-management/)
explains how to check before you book.

Some blocks are both at once: an occipital block that relieves a headache
helps confirm the diagnosis and treats it. That is fine, as long as someone
tells you which result they are looking for.

## Risks to hear about

Nerve blocks are generally low-risk, but not risk-free. Soreness and bruising
at the injection site are common. Numbness or weakness in the area can last a
few hours, so you may need a driver. Infection, bleeding, nerve injury and
reactions to the anesthetic are rare, and some blocks carry risks of their
own, which the clinician should explain. Steroids can raise blood sugar for
several days. Tell the clinician about blood thinners, diabetes, allergies and
any chance of pregnancy, and do not stop a medication on your own.

## Four questions to ask

1. Is this block a test or a treatment?
2. How much relief, for how long, will count as a good result?
3. What happens next if it works, and if it does not?
4. How many blocks do you expect, and when would we stop?

Nerve blocks for chronic pain are performed by interventional pain physicians.
You can [search our directory of pain clinics](/pain-clinics/) by location. As
