Call three pain clinics and you may be booked with three very different people:
an anesthesiologist at one, a physiatrist at the next, a nurse practitioner at
the third. All of them can be good choices. But they trained differently, and
that training shapes what they look for, what they offer first, and what they
are likely to recommend.

This guide explains who you may meet at a pain clinic, what each kind of
clinician is trained to do, and how to match the specialist to your problem.

## First, what "pain specialist" usually means

Pain medicine is a subspecialty, not a starting point. In the United States, a
physician typically finishes a residency in another field, then completes an
extra year of fellowship training in pain medicine. Several specialty boards
offer certification in pain medicine, including the boards for anesthesiology,
physical medicine and rehabilitation, and psychiatry and neurology.

So two doctors who both call themselves pain specialists can have the same
fellowship and very different backgrounds. That background is worth asking
about, because it tends to color everything else.

## The physicians you are most likely to meet

**Anesthesiologists with pain fellowship training.** This is the most common
path into interventional pain medicine. Anesthesiologists spend their residency
placing needles and catheters precisely, managing sedation and watching closely
for complications, so procedures are a natural fit. Expect a strong focus on
finding the specific structure causing your pain and treating it with
image-guided procedures: epidural steroid injections, nerve blocks,
radiofrequency ablation and spinal cord stimulator trials. Our guide to
[what interventional pain clinics do](/news/what-interventional-pain-clinics-do/)
covers those procedures in more detail.

**Physiatrists (physical medicine and rehabilitation).** The name confuses
people. A physiatrist is a medical doctor, not a physical therapist. Their
residency centers on function: how well you walk, lift, work and sleep, and
what is getting in the way. Many physiatrists perform the same spine
procedures anesthesiologists do. They also often do nerve conduction studies and
electromyography (EMG), which test whether a nerve is pinched or damaged. A
physiatrist is a good fit when the goal is not only less pain but getting back
to specific activities.

**Neurologists.** Neurologists treat disorders of the brain, spinal cord and
nerves. In pain care, they are most often the right specialist for headaches and
migraine, nerve damage from diabetes or other causes (peripheral neuropathy),
and conditions such as trigeminal neuralgia. Some complete pain fellowships and
work in pain clinics. Most general neurologists do not perform spine injections.

## Specialists you may be referred to, or from

Pain clinics sit in the middle of a referral network. Depending on your
problem, you may be sent from, or on to, any of these:

- **Orthopedic surgeons and neurosurgeons** evaluate whether a structural
  problem, such as a badly damaged joint or a compressed spinal cord, needs
  surgery. Many people see a pain specialist first and a surgeon only if
  conservative care fails, or the reverse.
- **Rheumatologists** treat inflammatory and autoimmune conditions such as
  rheumatoid arthritis, lupus and gout. Joint pain with morning stiffness,
  swelling or a family history of these conditions often belongs here first.
- **Psychologists and other behavioral health clinicians** teach skills such as
  cognitive behavioral therapy for pain. This is not a suggestion that the pain
  is "in your head." Long-running pain changes sleep, mood and how the nervous
  system processes signals, and these therapies have evidence behind them.
- **Physical and occupational therapists** build strength, movement and daily
  function. Most good pain plans include them.

## The rest of the clinic team

**Nurse practitioners and physician assistants** see many pain patients,
especially for follow-up visits and medication management. In most states they
work with a supervising or collaborating physician, and many are highly
experienced in pain care. It is reasonable to ask who the supervising physician
is and when you would see that doctor.

**Chiropractors and acupuncturists** appear in some multidisciplinary practices.
They are licensed in their own fields but are not medical physicians, and they
do not prescribe medication or perform injections.

## Matching the specialist to your problem

There is no single right answer, but some patterns hold:

- Back or neck pain traced to a specific spot, after physical therapy has not
  been enough: an interventional pain physician, often from anesthesiology or
  physical medicine and rehabilitation.
- Pain that mainly limits function, or numbness and weakness that need nerve
  testing: a physiatrist.
- Headaches, migraine or nerve pain from neuropathy: a neurologist, ideally one
  with a headache or pain focus.
- Swollen, stiff joints in several places: a rheumatologist.
- Long-standing, widespread pain affecting sleep and mood: a multidisciplinary
  team that includes behavioral health.

## How to check who you will actually see

Ask when you call:

- Who will I see at the first visit, a physician, nurse practitioner or
  physician assistant?
- What was the physician's residency, and did they complete a pain medicine
  fellowship?
- Is the physician board certified, and in what? You can confirm certification
  through the American Board of Medical Specialties' public lookup.
- Does the clinic perform procedures itself, or refer them out?

## How PainBeacon reads a clinic's specialty

Every provider in the United States has a National Provider Identifier, and
when they register, they declare one or more specialty codes. A practice
appears in our [directory of pain clinics](/pain-clinics/) when at least one of
its declared codes is a pain medicine code: interventional pain medicine,
general pain medicine, or the pain subspecialty of anesthesiology, physical
medicine and rehabilitation, or psychiatry and neurology.

Those codes are self-declared in a federal registry. They are a useful signal,
but they are not the same as board certification, which is why the questions
above still matter. Area pages let you narrow the list by discipline, such as
anesthesiology, physical medicine and rehab, neurology or physical therapy, and
each card names the practice type whenever pain medicine is not its primary
registration. In [our ranking](/how-we-rank/), carrying the interventional pain
medicine code is one of five published signals, and no clinic can pay for a
higher position.
