"Pill mill" is a blunt term for a specific thing: a practice organized around
volume prescribing rather than diagnosis. The overwhelming majority of pain
clinics are not this. But the ones that are tend to leave the same fingerprints,
and most of those fingerprints are visible before you book — on the website, in
one phone call, in the first ten minutes of a visit.

Being able to spot them matters for a reason beyond the obvious. Practices that
get shut down leave their patients stranded mid-treatment, sometimes on
medications that need a careful taper. Screening first is easier than
recovering later.

## What the website tells you

Start with the physicians. A legitimate practice names them, lists their
training, and lets you verify it. If you cannot find a single named physician —
only a practice name, a phone number, and a promise of relief — that absence is
the signal. Board certification in pain medicine through an ABMS member board
is checkable in public lookup tools, and the federal NPI registry shows how the
practice registered its own scope. Those are facts rather than marketing, which
is exactly why they carry weight in [our ranking
methodology](/how-we-rank/).

Then read what the practice advertises. Warning signs cluster around the
medication itself rather than the diagnosis:

- **Drugs named in the marketing.** Legitimate clinics advertise conditions and
  procedures. A site that leads with specific controlled substances, "same-day
  prescriptions," or "we will not reduce your dose" is selling a product.
- **Cash-only, or conspicuously no insurance information.** Careful documentation
  and insurance billing go together. A pain practice that takes no insurance at
  all deserves questions — see our guide to [what insurance actually
  covers](/news/does-insurance-cover-pain-management/).
- **No conditions, no workup, no procedures.** If nothing on the site describes
  how a diagnosis gets made, that step may not be happening.
- **Guaranteed outcomes.** Nobody can promise pain relief. Honest clinics
  describe odds, not certainties.

## The phone call test

One call to the front desk sorts most of the remainder. Ask four questions:

1. **Do you bill my insurance?**
2. **Will you request my records and imaging before the first visit?**
3. **Who will examine me — a physician, or a nurse practitioner or PA?** (Either
   can be fine; evasion is not.)
4. **What does the first visit involve?**

You are listening for a workup. "Bring your imaging, we will take a full
history, and the doctor will examine you before anyone discusses treatment" is
the right answer. "Bring your last prescription bottle and we will get you
taken care of" is not. A front office fields these questions all day —
vagueness is itself information.

## What the visit itself shows

If you get as far as the door, a few patterns are worth noticing. Federal
enforcement actions describe them repeatedly: very long lines with very short
appointments, patients arriving in groups or from surprising distances, a cash
window, security staffing that feels disproportionate to a medical office, and
no physical examination before a prescription is written. Add the tell from our
[guide to choosing a clinic](/news/how-to-choose-a-pain-clinic/) — the same
treatment for every patient regardless of what brought them in.

You are allowed to leave. Walking out of a first appointment costs you an
afternoon; it is not a medical decision.

## What looks strict but is actually good care

This is the part people get backwards, and it matters just as much. A clinic
being careful with controlled substances is not a clinic being difficult. All
of the following are ordinary, standard practice, broadly consistent with the
CDC's 2022 clinical practice guideline on opioid prescribing:

- Checking your state's prescription drug monitoring program before prescribing.
- Urine drug screening, pill counts, and a written controlled-substance agreement.
- Requiring physical therapy, non-opioid medication, or an injection trial
  before considering opioids.
- Declining to fill early refills, or refusing to inherit a dose it considers
  unsafe without its own workup.
- Proposing a gradual taper, or not prescribing opioids at all and referring you
  elsewhere for that part of your care.

None of that is a red flag. A clinic that skips all of it, on the other hand,
is skipping the guardrails that protect its patients — and, eventually, itself.

## If you think you have found one

Do not stop a prescribed medication abruptly on your own; some pain medications
require a supervised taper, and an unmanaged stop can be dangerous. Bring it to
your primary care physician or another pain specialist and ask them to take over
the prescribing and the records. Practice concerns can be reported to your
state medical board and, for prescribing specifically, to your state board of
pharmacy or the DEA's tip line — but getting your own care re-established
somewhere solid comes first.
