The worst part of a car accident is often not the accident. It is the second
morning, when the adrenaline is gone and your neck will not turn, or a week
later, when the ache in your lower back has started running down one leg. By
then the police report is filed, the other driver's insurer has already called,
and you are trying to work out whether this is something that will pass or
something that needs a specialist.

This guide is about that stretch: what crash pain usually is, when it belongs
in a pain clinic rather than a primary care office, and why the first few weeks
of care matter far beyond your recovery. Because there is a second track
running alongside the medical one, we asked a personal injury attorney who has
handled crash cases in Virginia and Maryland since 1980 to explain what the
paper trail looks like from his side of the desk.

## Why the pain arrives late

Delayed-onset pain after a collision is the norm, not the exception. The
mechanism is unglamorous: soft tissue in the neck and back is stretched and
strained in a fraction of a second, inflammation builds over the following 24 to
72 hours, and the stress response that carried you through the scene fades. The
result is that many people feel roughly fine at the roadside and considerably
worse two days later.

The common patterns:

- **Neck strain ("whiplash")** — stiffness, headache at the base of the skull,
  pain turning the head. Most improves over weeks with movement and simple
  measures.
- **Low back strain** — dull pain across the lower back, worse with sitting or
  standing up. Same trajectory, usually.
- **Radicular pain** — pain, tingling, or numbness that travels down an arm or
  leg. This is a nerve-root problem, not a muscle problem, and it is the pattern
  a pain clinic is built for.
- **Concussion symptoms** — headache, fog, light sensitivity, poor sleep. Not a
  pain-clinic problem first; it needs your primary care physician or a
  concussion clinic.

## Go to the emergency department if

Some symptoms are not a wait-and-see situation. Go to an emergency department
the same day for any of the following: numbness or weakness that is getting
worse, loss of bladder or bowel control, severe headache with confusion or
vomiting, chest or abdominal pain, or pain that came on with a loss of
consciousness at the scene. Those are red flags for injuries that imaging and
an emergency physician need to rule out first.

Everything else can start with your primary care physician or an urgent care,
ideally within the first 48 hours. That visit does two things: it gets a
clinician's eyes on you while the injury is fresh, and it creates the first
dated record that you were hurt.

## When it becomes a pain-clinic problem

Most crash-related strain improves inside four to six weeks with movement,
over-the-counter anti-inflammatories, and physical therapy. A pain medicine
clinic earns its place when the pattern breaks:

- Pain that has not clearly improved after four to six weeks of conservative
  care.
- Pain that travels — down an arm from the neck, or down a leg from the back —
  especially with tingling or numbness. That is the radicular pattern, and
  [epidural steroid injections](/news/epidural-steroid-injections-evidence/)
  have their best evidence there.
- Pain that is stopping you from doing physical therapy at all, so that the
  thing supposed to fix it cannot start.
- Headaches that persist after the neck has otherwise settled, which can be
  driven by the small facet joints of the upper neck.

What a good clinic does with a post-crash patient is diagnostic first: a full
history, an examination, a look at whatever imaging exists, and a plan that
usually combines physical therapy with targeted treatment for the specific
structure that is generating the pain. It should look like the workup we
describe in [what to expect at a first pain clinic
appointment](/news/first-pain-clinic-appointment/). A practice that offers the
same injection to everyone who walks in with a crash story is not a practice
you want to be in, whatever it promises about your case.

## The second track: the paper trail

Here is the part nobody explains in the emergency department. If another driver
caused the crash, their insurer will eventually be asked to pay for your care.
Whether it does, and how much, depends heavily on records that are being
created — or not created — in exactly the weeks when you are least inclined to
think about them.

Three habits that cost nothing and matter a great deal:

1. **See someone early and keep going.** A gap between the crash and the first
   visit, or a gap of weeks in the middle of treatment, is read by adjusters as
   evidence you were not really hurt. Follow the referral chain: primary care,
   then physical therapy, then a specialist if you are referred to one.
2. **Describe the pain accurately every time.** Say where it is, what it stops
   you doing, and whether it is better or worse. Do not minimize it to be
   polite, and do not inflate it. Consistency across visits is what a record
   is worth.
3. **Keep your own file.** Every visit date, every provider, every bill, every
   mileage log, every day of work missed. A folder or a phone note is enough.

## Three jurisdictions, three sets of rules

The Washington region is unusual in that a ten-minute drive can change the law
that applies to your crash. A few things worth knowing, all of which your
attorney should confirm for your specific situation:

- **Contributory negligence.** Virginia, Maryland, and the District of Columbia
  are among the last places in the country that still apply it: if you are
  found even slightly at fault, you may recover nothing from the other driver.
  [CONFIRM — Samakow. DC has a carve-out for pedestrians and cyclists; confirm
  the current scope.] This is the rule that makes early legal advice in the
  DMV matter more than it might elsewhere.
- **Deadlines.** Personal injury claims generally must be filed within two years
  in Virginia and three years in Maryland and DC, counted from the date of the
  crash. [CONFIRM — Samakow, including any shorter notice deadlines for claims
  involving a government vehicle.]
- **Who pays the medical bills first.** Depending on the state and your own
  policy, your medical bills may be paid initially by your own auto coverage
  (medical payments or personal injury protection), your health insurance, or
  neither until the claim resolves. Ask the clinic's billing office how it
  handles accident cases before your first visit; some practices will treat on
  a letter of protection from your attorney, and some will not.

## What to ask a clinic about accident cases

When you call, add three questions to the ones in our [guide to choosing a
clinic](/news/how-to-choose-a-pain-clinic/):

1. **Do you treat patients with an open auto-accident claim, and how do you bill
   for it?** (Med-pay, health insurance, letter of protection, or self-pay.)
2. **Will you provide complete records and an itemized bill on request?** You
   will need both; a clinic that hesitates is a clinic that will be a problem
   later.
3. **Will the physician write a narrative report if one is needed?** Some do,
   some charge for it, some refuse. Better to know now.

None of this changes the medical advice. Get seen early, follow through on
treatment, and let the specialist decide what your neck or back actually needs.
The paperwork is just the part that makes sure you are not the one paying for
someone else's mistake.
