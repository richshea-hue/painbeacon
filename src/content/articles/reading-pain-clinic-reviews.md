A pain clinic with 4.9 stars and a clinic with 4.3 stars are not the same, but
the gap may not mean what you think. Stars are the first thing most people look
at, and for good reason: they are quick, and they come from other patients. They
are also an unusually blunt tool in pain care, where the thing you most need to
know, whether the treatment plan is sound, is the thing a review is least able
to show.

This guide explains what a rating does tell you, where it misleads, and how to
read a clinic's reviews without being steered by a single number. It also
explains exactly how ratings figure in our own ranking, because we use them and
you should know how much weight they carry.

## What a star rating is good at

Reviews are strong evidence about the parts of a visit a patient can judge
directly:

- **Access.** How long the wait for an appointment was, and how long you sat in
  the waiting room once you arrived.
- **Courtesy.** Whether the front desk and clinical staff treated people with
  respect, and whether calls were returned.
- **Communication.** Whether the doctor listened, explained the plan in plain
  language and answered questions.
- **Billing and logistics.** Surprise charges, trouble with referrals or prior
  authorization, a phone line nobody answers.

A pattern in any of these is worth taking seriously. If a dozen reviewers
independently describe being rushed or ignored, believe them. Those are the
experiences you will have too.

## What a star rating cannot tell you

**Whether the treatment was right.** A patient can tell if the doctor was kind.
A patient usually cannot tell if the injection was placed at the correct level,
if the diagnosis was reasonable, or if a simpler option should have come first.
Technical quality is mostly invisible from the chair. A warm clinic that
over-treats can earn five stars, and a careful clinic that talks you out of a
procedure can earn three.

**Whether pain got better.** Chronic pain often changes slowly and unevenly.
Someone who writes a review the day after a visit has not yet seen how the plan
works. Someone who writes months later may be writing because it did not.

**Whether the clinic is safe.** Our guide to
[spotting a pill mill](/news/spot-a-pill-mill-red-flags/) describes practices
that hand out prescriptions with little examination. Some of those have
enthusiastic reviews from people who got exactly what they came for. Glowing
reviews are not a safety check.

**Whether the low ratings are fair.** Pain care carries a particular source of
anger. A patient who was declined a medication, or tapered from one, may leave
one star after an appointment that was clinically appropriate. That does not
make the review dishonest, but it is a different kind of complaint from "the
doctor never examined me."

## The small-numbers problem

A rating is an average, and an average of very few numbers is fragile.

Three reviews averaging 5.0 tell you less than two hundred averaging 4.6. With
three, one friendly colleague or one angry afternoon moves the result by a full
star. With hundreds, individual outliers wash out and the number settles on
something real.

That is why a clinic's review count matters at least as much as its rating. When
you compare two clinics, look at both numbers together. A slightly lower rating
over many reviews is usually the more trustworthy signal.

## How ratings figure in our ranking

We use ratings, but we limit how far they can move a clinic. The scoring in
[how we rank pain clinics](/news/how-we-rank-pain-clinics/) and on our
[methodology page](/how-we-rank/) works like this, and it matches the code:

- **Rating is 25 of 100 points.** The practice's Google rating is scaled linearly from
  zero to five stars. The difference between a 4.0 and a 4.5 is therefore about
  2.5 points out of 100, not a leap in the order.
- **Google review volume is another 20 points.** It is scaled logarithmically and
  capped at around 200 reviews, so a few reviews count for something, many count
  for more, and past about 200 more reviews add nothing.
- **The remaining 55 points come from other signals:** whether the practice
  is registered under interventional pain medicine in the federal provider
  registry (a self-declared code, not a board certification), how long its
  national provider record has existed, and whether it publishes a website and phone number.

So reviews and ratings together account for under half of a clinic's score. A
clinic with a perfect rating and no interventional registration does not
automatically outrank an established interventional practice with a 4.4. That
is deliberate, for the reasons above: the stars measure experience, and the
other signals are checkable facts.

Many clinics in the directory have no rating we can read. A "basic listing" says
we hold little verified data on the practice. It is not a verdict on the care.

## How to read a clinic's reviews

1. **Check the count before the rating.** Treat a rating built on fewer than
   about ten reviews as a hint, not a finding.
2. **Read the middle.** Skip the five-star one-liners and the furious
   one-star posts. Three- and four-star reviews tend to be the most specific
   about what was good and what was not.
3. **Look for repeats, not outliers.** One complaint is an anecdote. The same
   complaint across many months is a pattern.
4. **Sort by newest.** Staff and doctors change. A glowing history can predate
   a new owner.
5. **Notice what the praise is about.** Praise for friendly staff is useful.
   Praise that centers on how easy it was to get a particular prescription
   deserves a second look.
6. **Notice how the clinic responds.** A calm, specific reply to a bad review
   says something. A defensive one, or a pile of identical copy-and-paste
   replies, says something too.
7. **Be careful with the extremes.** A rating of exactly 5.0 across many
   reviews is rare for any practice. Dozens of similar-sounding reviews posted
   in a short burst are a reason to look elsewhere.

## Pair the reviews with things you can verify

Reviews tell you how a clinic feels to deal with. They should sit beside facts
you can check yourself:

- **Credentials.** Is the physician board-certified in pain medicine or in a
  related specialty? Our guide to
  [who's who at a pain clinic](/news/pain-clinic-specialists-who-is-who/)
  explains the training backgrounds you will see.
- **Scope.** Does the clinic offer a range of options, not only one kind of
  treatment? Our [choosing a clinic guide](/news/how-to-choose-a-pain-clinic/)
  covers what to look for.
- **The conversation.** Whatever the stars say, ask the questions in
  [what to ask before agreeing to any pain procedure](/news/questions-to-ask-before-a-pain-procedure/).
  A clinic that welcomes them is a good sign, and no review can tell you that.

## The bottom line

Use stars to screen out clinics with consistent problems with access,
communication or billing, and use review counts to decide how much to trust
the number. Do not use them to decide whether a treatment is right for you.
That judgment belongs to a qualified clinician, a clear explanation of the
options, and your own comfort with the plan.
