Somewhere between the insurance forms and the first appointment, a lot of pain
clinics hand you a second clipboard. Nine questions about how often you have
felt down, hopeless, uninterested in things. Seven more about worrying. You came
in about your back.

Most people read that clipboard the same way, and it is not a generous reading:
*they think I am making this up.*

That is not what it is for. But the reassurance only works if you know what the
questions are actually doing, so here is the honest version — including the part
where a psychological evaluation really can stand between you and a procedure,
which nobody mentions up front.

## Pain and mood are not two separate systems

The split we all carry around — physical pain over here, mental health over
there — is a filing convention, not a fact about the body. The circuits that
carry a pain signal and the circuits that set mood and attention overlap, and
they run in both directions.

Three pieces of that are worth knowing, because each one is something a clinic
can act on.

**Sleep sits in the middle of it.** Pain wrecks sleep, and short or broken sleep
measurably lowers the threshold at which the nervous system registers pain the
next day. That is a loop that tightens on its own, and it is one of the few
places where a treatment aimed at the "mental health" side produces a change you
can feel in the pain itself.

**A nervous system can learn pain.** When pain signaling continues for months,
the system handling it becomes more sensitive rather than less — the volume knob
gets turned up and stays up. That is a physical change in a physical system, and
it is a large part of why pain can persist long after the original injury has
healed, and why an imaging scan can look unremarkable while the pain is entirely
real.

**Depression and anxiety are common here, and the traffic runs both ways.**
Studies in pain clinic populations routinely find depression at rates far above
the general population — the figures vary a lot by setting and by how the
screening is done, so treat any single number you see with suspicion. The
direction is the useful part: untreated low mood makes pain harder to live with
and harder to treat, and persistent pain drags mood down. Neither is the cause
of the other. They feed each other.

None of this means the pain is psychological. It means the thing in pain is
attached to a brain, and the brain is part of the mechanism.

## So what is the questionnaire for

Usually it is the PHQ-9 and the GAD-7, two short screening tools used across all
of medicine. In a pain clinic they do three ordinary jobs.

They **catch the treatable thing sitting underneath the pain**, because a
depression that nobody has named will quietly undo a good treatment plan. They
**set a baseline**, so that six months from now there is something to compare
against other than memory. And they **steer the plan** — a patient who is barely
sleeping and a patient who is sleeping fine get different first moves, even with
identical scans.

Answer them honestly. A low score bought by under-reporting buys you nothing; it
just removes the parts of the plan that might have worked.

## The part nobody warns you about

For most of what a pain clinic does, these questionnaires change the shape of
your treatment and nothing else. There is one real exception, and it is better
heard now than at the time.

Before an implanted device — a spinal cord stimulator, an intrathecal pump —
most insurers and most implanting physicians require a **psychological
evaluation**, and it is a genuine gate. People are turned down. Our guide to
[what a spinal cord stimulator trial involves](/news/spinal-cord-stimulator-trial/)
covers where it sits in the sequence.

It is worth understanding what that evaluation is looking for, because it is not
looking for whether you are "mentally strong enough." It is looking for
untreated depression or anxiety severe enough to flatten the result, for
substance use that would complicate the surgery, and for expectations about the
outcome that the device cannot meet. Most of what gets flagged is treatable, and
a deferral is usually "not yet" rather than "no." Treat it as a step with a
pass mark rather than a verdict on you.

## What actually helps

This is the part that gets sold badly. "Have you tried therapy" lands, to
somebody in pain, as a change of subject. Done properly it is not one, and the
evidence is better than its reputation — with the honest caveat that the effects
are moderate. Nothing below is a cure for pain. They are good tools for the
problem of living a life while it is happening, and several of them lower pain
scores as a side effect of that.

**Cognitive behavioral therapy for chronic pain** is the best-evidenced of them.
It is skills training, not talking about your childhood: pacing activity so you
stop the boom-and-bust cycle, working on the thought patterns that reliably
amplify a flare, rebuilding activity in graded steps. Typically eight to sixteen
sessions, with homework. The target is what pain costs you, and the pain score
tends to follow part of the way.

**Acceptance and commitment therapy** takes a different angle at the same
problem — less about changing the thoughts, more about loosening their grip
while you go and do the things that matter to you anyway. It has a solid
evidence base in chronic pain, and it tends to suit people who have been told to
"think positive" one time too many.

**CBT for insomnia** is the one I would put first if sleep is broken, and it is
the one most often skipped. It outperforms sleeping pills for chronic insomnia
and it does not stop working when you stop it. Given the sleep-pain loop above,
it is frequently the highest-leverage thing on this list.

**Mindfulness-based programs** have real but modest effects on pain-related
distress. Useful. Not magic, and the eight-week group format asks a lot of
somebody who is struggling to get out of the house.

**Pain neuroscience education** — being taught, properly, how a sensitized
nervous system works — is a small intervention with a decent evidence base,
usually as part of something larger rather than on its own. Newer approaches
built on it, including pain reprocessing therapy, have produced genuinely
striking trial results in specific populations. They are promising and not yet
settled, and anyone selling them to you as a cure is ahead of the evidence.

**And the medication overlap is real.** Duloxetine, amitriptyline and
nortriptyline are antidepressants that are also standard nerve-pain drugs,
prescribed at doses and for reasons that have nothing to do with mood. Being
offered one is not a hint about what your doctor believes, a point our guide to
[pain management without opioids](/news/pain-management-without-opioids/) makes
at more length.

## If a clinician really is dismissing you

Sometimes the fear is accurate. Some clinicians do use "it's stress" as a
stopping point rather than a finding, and being disbelieved about your own body
is its own injury.

The tell is the difference between **adding** and **substituting**. A clinician
who says "the pain is real, the nervous system is part of the mechanism, and
here is what we are doing about the pain *and* here is a referral" is treating
you properly. A clinician who offers the referral *in place of* a workup, or who
stops investigating because a questionnaire scored high, is not.

What to say, plainly: *I am not refusing the referral. I want to know what the
plan is for the pain itself.* If there is no answer to that, you have learned
something useful about the practice, and our guide to
[choosing a pain clinic](/news/how-to-choose-a-pain-clinic/) covers what to look
for in the next one.

## Finding a therapist who knows this work

Not every good therapist is the right one here, and the difference is specific
rather than a matter of quality.

Ask whether they work with **chronic illness and chronic pain specifically** —
health psychology, behavioral medicine, pain rehabilitation. Ask whether they
use **CBT, ACT or CBT-I**, by name; a therapist who does this work will answer
without hesitating. Ask how they **coordinate with your pain clinic**, because
the arrangement works much better when the two are speaking. And ask about
**insurance and telehealth**, because the practical barriers are what usually
end these plans — a program you can attend from home on a bad day is worth more
than a better one across town that you will stop attending.

## When it is more urgent than that

Chronic pain is an established risk factor for suicide, and the people most at
risk are often the ones least likely to raise it, because they have learned that
describing how bad it is gets them labeled.

If you are thinking about ending your life, that is a medical emergency and not
a character flaw. In the United States, call or text **988** for the Suicide and
Crisis Lifeline, any hour. Tell a clinician on your care team as well. It will
not cost you your pain treatment, and it is exactly the thing the questionnaire
at the front desk was trying to find.

## Finding a clinic that works this way

The tell is whether the behavioral side is in the building or in the plan. A
practice with a psychologist on staff, or with real referral relationships and a
habit of using them, is set up to treat the whole mechanism. A practice that
hands out the questionnaire, files it, and never mentions it again is collecting
data rather than using it.

It is a fair question to ask on the phone before you book: *who do you work with
on the behavioral side, and when do you bring them in?* Our
[directory](/pain-clinics/) and [methodology](/how-we-rank/) can tell you what a
practice's credentials and record look like; neither can tell you how a clinic
answers that question. Asking it takes one phone call.
