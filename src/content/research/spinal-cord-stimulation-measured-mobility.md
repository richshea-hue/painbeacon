abstract: "Objective: Spinal cord stimulation (SCS) is a treatment option for chronic neuropathic pain conditions when conventional therapies have failed. However, objective and measurable long-term data on the effects of SCS are lacking. This study evaluates changes in objectively recorded mobility and the correlation with patient-reported outcomes in SCS-treated patients with intractable back and/or leg pain following lumbar spine surgery.\n\nMethods: Fifty patients were enrolled. Baseline mobility was recorded over 4 weeks using an external neurostimulator with a built-in accelerometer. Patients achieving ≥50% pain relief during a subsequent SCS test trial received a permanently implanted stimulator at the same site. Mobility data were extracted at 3, 6, and 12 months and compared with baseline alongside work status, medication usage, and patient-reported outcome measures.\n\nResults: Thirty-seven patients received a permanent stimulator, and 32 completed the 12 month follow-up of activity data. Time spent in mobility episodes lasting at least 30 s was 34±27 min/24 hours for SCS-patients at baseline compared with 92±51 min for healthy controls (p=0.009). Mobility increased gradually after SCS, reaching statistical significance at 12 months (p=0.045). The increase was significant in patients with predominant leg pain (35±27 to 54±32 min, p=0.011) but not in those with predominant back pain. Significant improvements were observed in working ability, medication reduction, and self-reported outcomes. Increased mobility correlated significantly with reduction in self-reported disability (p=0.044) and leg pain (p=0.046).\n\nConclusion: The new objective data indicate that SCS has beneficial, long-term effects on mobility in patients with intractable leg pain after spine surgery.\n\nTrial registration number: NCT04710355." # us-english-ok

**What the study looked at.** Most research on spinal cord stimulators, implanted devices that send mild electrical pulses to the spinal cord, relies on what patients report in questionnaires. This study added a measurement that does not: the stimulator used has a built-in motion sensor. Researchers at seven hospitals in Belgium, Denmark, the Netherlands and Sweden enrolled 50 adults who had had back or leg pain for at least six months after lumbar spine surgery. Each wore an external sensor for four weeks before treatment to set a baseline. Those who had at least 50% pain relief during a two-week test of the stimulator received a permanent implant, and the sensor kept recording for 12 months.

**What it found.**

- Of the 50 people enrolled, 45 had the two-week test, 37 received a permanent stimulator, and 32 had a full year of movement data.
- Before treatment, patients spent about 34 minutes a day in stretches of movement lasting at least 30 seconds. Five healthy volunteers measured the same way averaged 92 minutes.
- At 12 months, the patients averaged 44 minutes a day, a 30% increase. The change was gradual and reached statistical significance only at 12 months.
- The gain was concentrated in people whose leg pain was worse than their back pain: from 35 to 54 minutes a day. People with mostly back pain showed no significant change in movement.
- Reported pain fell. On a 0-to-100 scale, average worst leg pain went from 80.7 to 42.1 and worst back pain from 75.7 to 40.9, with a similar reduction whichever pain was worse.
- Eight of the 50 enrolled patients (16%) had a complication. Two developed infections during the test period and three needed repeat surgery for a lead that malfunctioned or moved.

**What it could not show.** Whether the stimulator caused the change. The authors name the lack of a comparison group of untreated patients as the study's main limitation. They also note that no one has established how large an increase in this measure matters to a patient, so it is unclear whether ten more minutes a day is a meaningful improvement. The group was small, and only patients who responded to the test went on to an implant. The authors themselves call for more studies without industry involvement.

**Why it matters for patients.** A lower pain score does not always mean a person is doing more, and this study measured both: in people with mostly back pain, reported pain fell but measured movement did not change. The results apply to adults with persistent pain after lumbar spine surgery who responded to a test of the device. They do not speak to other conditions, or to how stimulation compares with other treatments or with no treatment.

If a stimulator has been suggested to you, it is reasonable to ask what the test period is meant to show, which results the clinic tracks beyond pain scores, and how often leads need to be revised.
