**What the study looked at.** Researchers used Medicaid insurance records from 25 states, covering 2016 through 2019, to follow 399,500 adults under 65 who had just been diagnosed with acute low back pain. All of them were "opioid-naive" (not already taking opioids) and all received some treatment within a month of the diagnosis. The researchers recorded what each person's first treatments were, including medications, physical therapy and chiropractic care, then counted how many went on to receive a new diagnosis of opioid use disorder or opioid overdose over the following 12 months. This is an analysis of insurance records, not a trial: nobody was assigned to a treatment.

**What it found.**

- By 13 months, 2.3% of patients had a diagnosis of opioid use disorder or overdose (1.4% by 7 months).
- Starting with a higher-dose opioid, more than 50 milligrams of morphine equivalent a day, was linked to the largest increase: about 2.5 times the risk of people who did not start one.
- A lower-dose opioid prescribed for more than 7 days was linked to about twice the risk. So were gabapentinoids (gabapentin and pregabalin), which are sometimes thought of as a safer alternative.
- A lower-dose opioid for 7 days or fewer was linked to a much smaller increase, about 31%.
- Starting with chiropractic care was linked to about 33% lower risk, and physical therapy to about 31% lower risk.
- Most first treatments were medications. Opioids were part of the first treatment for 21% of patients, while 16% started physical therapy and 10% started chiropractic care. Fewer than half received any non-drug therapy at the start.

**What it could not show.** Cause and effect. The authors say so plainly: insurance records do not capture how severe a person's pain was or how well they could function, so the groups may have differed in ways the analysis could not see. Diagnosis codes can misclassify people, over-the-counter and cash-paid medications were invisible to the study, and the results may not apply to people who are not on Medicaid.

**Why it matters for patients.** Guidelines from the American College of Physicians already recommend non-drug treatments first for a new episode of low back pain. This study adds a second reason beyond pain relief: in a very large group of patients, how treatment started was associated with what happened a year later. It does not mean an opioid prescription causes addiction, or that nobody should take one. The authors themselves write that medications can be considered after weighing benefits against longer-term risks, and that when opioids are prescribed, keeping the dose and duration low remains prudent.

If you are offered a prescription for new back pain, it is reasonable to ask what non-drug options are available and how long the prescription is meant to last. Our guide to [pain management without opioids](/news/pain-management-without-opioids/) explains what those options are. Keep in mind that this study is about new, short-term back pain. It does not speak to people already being treated for chronic pain.
